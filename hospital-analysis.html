Hospital Patient Experience (HCAHPS) Analysis

HCAHPS is the national, standardized survey that asks hospital patients about a recent inpatient stay. I worked with state and national results from 2013 to 2021, across 10 care measures, to answer four questions: did hospitals improve, where did they improve most, what still needs work, and what should they do next.

01 · the data

One survey, every state, nine years.

HCAHPS asks discharged patients to rate their hospital stay. Answers fall into three boxes per measure: bottom-box (the worst ratings), middle-box, and top-box (the best ratings, such as 9 or 10 out of 10, or Always). This analysis tracks the top-box share: the percentage of patients giving the best possible answer.

The working data has two levels. National results hold 90 rows: 9 yearly releases times 10 measures. State results hold 4,580 rows covering 50 states plus DC, with zero duplicates. The 10 measures cover communication with nurses and doctors, staff responsiveness, medicines communication, discharge information, care transition, cleanliness, quietness, overall rating, and willingness to recommend.

One detail matters for reading every chart below. Each yearly release covers hospital stays ending the prior September, so the 2021 figures describe care delivered during the pandemic year.

question 01 · did hospitals improve

Yes, until 2019. Then COVID reversed the gains.

The national average top-box score rose every year from 70.83% in 2013 to 72.24% in 2019, a gain of 1.4 points built slowly over six years. Then it fell to 70.65% in 2020 and 69.42% in 2021, a drop of 2.8 points in two years. The nine-year net is negative: 2021 finished 1.4 points below where 2013 started, wiping out six years of steady progress.

question 02 · where progress was fastest

Responsiveness and overall rating gained most before COVID.

Comparing 2013 with the 2019 peak, every measure improved, but not equally. Staff responsiveness rose 2.3 points (68.1 to 70.4) and overall hospital rating rose 2.3 points (70.5 to 72.8). Cleanliness gained 2.0 points. At the other end, communication with doctors moved only 0.2 points and quietness 0.4 points, though both started from very different places: doctors near the top at 81.7, quietness near the bottom at 61.2.

question 03 · where the gaps are

Care transitions score lowest and fell farthest.

In 2021, Care Transition sat at 51.0%, nearly 35 points below Discharge Information at 85.7%. Quietness (61.1%) and medicines communication (61.5%) sit next lowest. The same measures also fell hardest during COVID: responsiveness dropped 4.9 points from 2019 to 2021, medicines communication 4.6, and cleanliness 3.9. Only quietness held roughly steady. The lowest scores and the steepest falls point at the same places, which makes the priority list short.

The gap between states is wide

Averaged across all nine years, South Dakota leads at 76.8% while the District of Columbia trails at 62.4%, a gap of 14.4 points. The top ten is filled with Midwestern states, while the bottom ten clusters on the coasts. Regions tell the same story: West North Central averages 74.9% against 67.3% in the Mid-Atlantic. Every region fell between 2019 and 2021, with New England dropping most at 3.7 points.

question 04 · recommendations

Five actions drawn straight from the numbers.

  1. Rebuild responsiveness first. It gained the most before COVID and lost the most during it (-4.9 points). Call bell answer times and nurse rounding routines deserve the first investment, since responsiveness also lifts overall ratings.
  2. Fix medicines communication. At 61.5% with a 4.6-point COVID drop, explaining medicines in plain terms at the bedside is the second priority. Pharmacist rounds and take-home instruction sheets target this directly.
  3. Redesign the discharge handoff. Care Transition scores lowest at 51.0%: patients leave unsure about medicines, warning signs, and follow-up. Structured discharge calls within 48 hours address exactly this measure.
  4. Study the Midwest, support the coasts. South Dakota, Nebraska, and Kansas lead by wide margins while DC, New Jersey, and New York trail. Peer learning between systems beats generic targets, and support should flow to Mid-Atlantic and South Atlantic hospitals first.
  5. Protect quietness and cleanliness routines. Quietness barely moved through the whole period, which means night noise programs never took hold. Cleanliness fell 3.9 points during COVID and has the furthest to climb back.
the bottom line

Hospitals improved for six years, then COVID took more.

Average top-box scores rose from 70.8% to 72.2% between 2013 and 2019, then fell to 69.4% by 2021. Responsiveness gained most and lost most. Care transitions, medicines communication, and quietness remain the weakest measures, and the gap between the best and worst states spans 14.4 points. The path back runs through staffing, discharge handoffs, and learning from the hospitals that stayed on top.

Measure every year, protect what improved, and fix what the crisis exposed.
