Bank Customer Churn Prediction & Retention Analysis

Customer churn is one of the most expensive problems for a retail bank. Losing a customer does not only mean losing an account. It can also mean losing deposits, product revenue, transaction activity, and future opportunities to deepen the relationship.

In this project, I analyzed 10,000 bank customers across France, Germany, and Spain to understand why customers leave, identify high-risk customer segments, and build a machine learning model capable of predicting churn before an account is closed. The final objective was not simply to achieve a high model accuracy. It was to create a model that helps a bank identify customers who may need attention and use those predictions to support targeted retention strategies.

chapter 01

Understanding the Customer Base

The first stage of the analysis was to understand who the bank's customers were. The dataset contained demographic, financial, geographic, and behavioral information including:

  • Credit Score, Gender, Age, Tenure, Balance
  • Number of Products, Credit Card ownership, Active membership
  • Estimated Salary, Geography, Churn status

The customer base was distributed across three countries:

CountryShare of Customers
France50.1%
Germany25.1%

France represented approximately half of the customer base, while Germany and Spain each represented approximately one quarter. The gender distribution was relatively balanced, although males represented a slightly larger proportion of customers.

The median customer age was 37 years, while the mean was approximately 38.9 years. Average estimated salary was similar across the three countries.

At first glance, the bank appeared to have a relatively stable customer base. However, comparing retained and churned customers revealed much more important differences.

chapter 02

What Does a Churned Customer Look Like?

I separated customers into two groups (Retained = 0 and Churned = 1), then compared the characteristics of both groups. Three patterns stood out.

Age

Retained customers had an average age of approximately 37.4 years. Churned customers averaged 44.8 years, a difference of approximately 7.4 years.

This does not mean age itself causes churn. Instead, it suggests that age is an important variable associated with customer behavior and should be considered when identifying customers at risk. For the bank, this could mean that older customers require different products, services, or relationship-management strategies than younger customers.

Account Balance

Churned customers had an average balance of approximately $91,109, compared with $72,745 for retained customers.

This was particularly important from a business perspective. The bank is not simply losing customers with small balances. It is potentially losing customers holding substantially more money. A customer with a high balance may represent a significantly greater financial opportunity or risk than a low-balance customer. Therefore, churn prediction can be used not only to identify who might leave, but also to help prioritize which customers should receive retention attention first.

Customer Activity

One of the strongest behavioral differences was customer activity. Approximately 55% of retained customers were active, compared with only 36% of churned customers.

This suggests a strong relationship between engagement and churn. From a business perspective, this is particularly useful because activity is something a bank can potentially influence. Instead of waiting until a customer closes their account, the bank could monitor declining engagement and trigger an earlier intervention.

chapter 03

The Geographic Divide

The country-level analysis revealed an even more significant difference:

France and Spain showed very similar patterns. Approximately half of customers in both countries had zero balances, and their churn rates were around 16%.

Germany was very different. German customers had an average balance of approximately $119,730, almost twice the average observed in France and Spain, while churning at 32.4%. Germany represents a smaller proportion of the customer base than France, but the customers who are leaving appear to represent considerably more deposited capital.

The data does not tell us exactly why German customers churn at a higher rate. Possible explanations could include differences in product preferences, customer expectations, competition, service experience, or other factors not captured in the dataset. Rather than assuming the cause, I treat Germany as a priority market for further investigation and retention analysis.

Multi-Product At-Risk

The smallest but most concerning segment. Only 326 customers belonged to this group, but its churn rate was approximately 85.9%. Rather than assuming that owning multiple products causes churn, the finding could indicate problems with product bundling, customer-product fit, fees, service experience, or another factor not captured by the available variables. For the bank, this segment would warrant immediate investigation.

Older Single-Product Customers

This segment contained 2,231 customers, making it one of the largest high-risk groups, with a churn rate of approximately 45.2%. Because the segment is large, even a modest improvement in retention could prevent a substantial number of account closures. Possible retention strategies could include relationship-management programs, retirement planning services, savings products, or personalized financial advice.

Inactive German Customers

This segment contained 819 customers with a churn rate of approximately 22.2%. The combination of German geography, inactivity, and the country's high average account balance makes this segment particularly important. A bank could use customer activity signals to identify these customers before they reach the point of account closure.

chapter 05

Preparing the Data for Machine Learning

After completing the exploratory analysis, I moved from understanding churn to predicting it. The target variable was Exited, where 0 = Retained and 1 = Churned.

Encoding Categorical Variables

Gender was converted into a numerical representation. Geography was one-hot encoded into separate binary columns (Geography_Germany and Geography_Spain), with France as the reference category:

Feature Scaling

Numerical variables were placed on a comparable scale before model training. Critically, the scaler was fitted only on the training data, then used to transform the test data, preventing information from the test set from influencing preprocessing.

The scaler was saved alongside the final model so the exact same preprocessing is applied to new customers. The prediction pipeline is therefore:

chapter 07

Comparing Machine Learning Models

I did not want to select a model simply because it produced the highest accuracy. The business objective is to identify customers who are likely to churn, and because churn is the minority class, accuracy alone can be misleading. A model could score well by predicting that most customers stay, while missing the customers the bank actually needs to contact.

I therefore focused on precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, and the confusion matrix. For the churned class, recall is especially important: a false negative means the model predicted a customer would stay, but the customer actually churned, leaving without ever receiving a retention intervention.

The model correctly identified 274 of the 407 churned customers. Although CatBoost had better churn recall, Random Forest provided a better overall balance between precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy, and that distinction matters in a real banking environment.

If the model flags too many customers who were never going to leave, the bank wastes money on unnecessary discounts, incentives, calls, and relationship-management resources. If it is too conservative, the bank fails to identify valuable customers before they leave. Random Forest offered the more balanced compromise, so I selected it as the final model.

chapter 10

Why Accuracy Was Not Enough

The final model achieved 84% accuracy, but accuracy was not the main reason for selecting it. The dataset is imbalanced, with retained customers significantly outnumbering churned customers. The more important results were churn precision of 60%, churn recall of 67%, and churn F1-score of 63%: the model identified approximately two-thirds of the customers who actually churned, and about 60% of flagged customers were actual churners.

In a real bank, the prediction threshold would not necessarily remain fixed. It would move with the cost of intervention. If retention campaigns are inexpensive, prioritize recall and catch more at-risk customers. If they are expensive, prioritize precision and focus on the strongest churn probabilities. The model therefore provides a risk probability that supports different business strategies, not just a yes/no prediction.

chapter 11

From Prediction to Business Action

The purpose of the model is not to predict churn for the sake of prediction. The real value comes from what the bank does with the prediction.

High churn probability + high balance

Prioritize these customers for immediate relationship-manager intervention, protecting valuable deposits and understanding the dissatisfaction.

High churn probability + low activity

Trigger a re-engagement campaign:

  • Personalized digital banking offers
  • Product education
  • Relationship-manager contact
  • Relevant financial products
  • Service feedback requests

High churn probability + older + single product

Offer personalized financial planning and relationship-management services to increase engagement and uncover unmet financial needs.

High churn probability + Germany

Given the higher churn rate and higher average balance among German customers, these predictions receive additional priority, alongside investigation into whether product offerings, pricing, digital services, or customer experience differ from France and Spain.

chapter 12

Deployment

To make the model usable beyond the notebook, I built a Streamlit application called Customer Retention Intelligence. It loads the saved random_forest_model.pkl and scaler.pkl, processes customer information with the same feature structure and preprocessing used during training, and returns a churn prediction, churn probability, risk category, and retention recommendation, turning the model into a decision-support tool rather than leaving it inside a Jupyter notebook.

executive conclusion

Churn is concentrated, so retention can be targeted.

The analysis showed that bank churn is not evenly distributed: older age, higher balances, lower activity, German geography, and certain product combinations were all strongly associated with higher churn, and small segments carried disproportionately high risk. These patterns proved predictable before account closure.

After comparing classifiers, Random Forest provided the best balance for the business objective at 84% accuracy and a 63% F1-score for churned customers.

Can the bank use the predictions to decide which customers need attention, and do so in a way that makes financial sense?

That is where the analysis moves from machine learning into business decision-making.
