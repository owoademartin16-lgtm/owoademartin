Nigerian Delivery Operations Analysis

Out of 150,000 parcels tracked across 15 Nigerian cities, 134,945 arrived safely and 15,055 did not. That headline hides the real story: seven couriers separated by less than one point, a 12-hour delivery ceiling no parcel ever crosses, routes whose averages differ by four minutes, and cities where the best courier changes every time. I rebuilt this picture from raw records in PostgreSQL to find where operations actually strain.

NoteThis dataset is synthetic. It names real logistics companies, but the numbers describe generated records, not their actual performance. Every finding below is a pattern in this data, and the value here is the analytical workflow: querying, validation, comparison, and honest limits.
01 · the data

150,000 rows, 13 columns, zero gaps.

The data comes from a public Hugging Face dataset of simulated Nigerian e-commerce deliveries, loaded into PostgreSQL as public.delivery_data. Each row carries delivery and order ids, courier and company, pickup and delivery timestamps, duration in minutes, distance, cities, status, rating, and fee. Validation passed clean: no duplicate delivery ids, no duplicate rows, zero missing values in any column, and spot-checked durations match their pickup-to-delivery timestamps.

03 · couriers compared

Seven couriers, separated by 0.65 points.

Ace Couriers leads on success at 90.16% and Red Star Express trails at 89.51%. Every other company lands between them. On failures the order mirrors itself: Kwik Delivery lowest at 7.90%, Red Star highest at 8.22%. Each company handled a similar workload of roughly 21,000 to 21,600 deliveries, so the comparison is fair on volume. The gaps are small enough that no company stands meaningfully apart in this data.

04 · how long it takes

Half the parcels take over six hours. None take over twelve.

Durations run from 60 minutes to exactly 720, with half the parcels past six hours (74,755) and a third past eight (49,931). A hard ceiling at 12 hours with zero crossings looks less like operations and more like a generation rule, and it deserves a flag before anyone plans around it.

Distance barely moves the needle

Grouped into five distance bands of about 30,000 deliveries each, average duration runs 388.79, 389.28, 389.49, 391.33, and 389.85 minutes from shortest to longest trips. The full spread is 2.5 minutes on a six-and-a-half-hour average. Within this data, how far a parcel travels tells you almost nothing about how long it takes.

Long routes cluster within four minutes

Among city pairs with at least 100 deliveries, Abuja to Abeokuta averages longest at 405.94 minutes over 653 trips. The next fourteen run down to 401.97, a spread of 3.97 minutes. Same-city legs appear too, such as Ibadan to Ibadan at 404.32, and belong in their own group in any serious routing review.

05 · ratings by outcome

Ratings barely move with the outcome.

Delivered averages 4.14, failed 4.13, returned 4.15: a total spread of 0.02 points. That flatness is itself a finding, but it comes with a warning. Unless ratings are collected the same way for every outcome, comparing a failed delivery score with a delivered one may compare two different things.

06 · failure by city

Onitsha fails most at 8.53%. Three cities tie lowest at 7.71%.

The full spread runs less than one point, from Onitsha at 8.53% down through Aba at 8.49% and Calabar at 8.43% to a three-way tie at 7.71% between Kaduna, Ilorin, and Ibadan. These ranks flag locations for deeper checks, not verdicts on the cities themselves.

The best courier changes by city

No company leads everywhere. Ace Couriers takes Lagos at 5.89% and Abuja at 6.80%, Kwik Delivery takes Ilorin at 6.50%, Dispatch Riders takes Enugu at 6.86%, and Red Star Express, weakest overall, takes Onitsha at 7.52% and Port Harcourt at 6.93%. Company averages hide these local differences, which is why the comparison has to happen city by city. The single worst cell in the data is DHL Nigeria in Onitsha at 9.60%.

the bottom line

Compare locally, distrust averages.

This project ran PostgreSQL aggregations over 150,000 delivery records: an 89.96% success rate, company gaps under one point, durations capped at 12 hours, routes clustered within four minutes, ratings flat across outcomes, and city failure rates inside a single point. The company-by-city table shows why one overall number per courier misleads: the best performer changes with the pickup city.

None of this describes real courier performance. The data is synthetic, and the honest output is the method: validate first, compare at the right level, and state the limits beside every number. That discipline transfers directly to real operational data.

Small gaps deserve small claims. Check the level, check the coverage, then speak.
