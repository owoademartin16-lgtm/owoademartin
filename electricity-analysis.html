Electricity Load Forecasting

Power grids cannot store electricity at scale, so supply must match demand every hour. I worked with 157,666 hourly load readings from the ENTSO-E European power data portal (January to June 2026) to learn the daily and weekly rhythms of demand, then built a model that predicts the next hour's load. It misses by about 1,774 MW on average.

01 · the data

Every hour, every zone, six months.

The file comes from the ENTSO-E (European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity) data portal: monthly hourly load values for 2026. Each row marks one hour in one bidding zone or country with three facts: the timestamp in UTC, the country or zone code such as DE or FR, and the metered load in megawatts. Coverage reporting sits at 100% across all rows.

Cleaning was light because the source is well kept. Sixty zero readings and 4 duplicate rows were removed. Building the history features then cost the first 168 hours, leaving 157,434 model-ready rows. Loads vary enormously by zone size, from under 1 MW to nearly 90,000 MW, with an overall mean of 9,125 MW and a median of 4,386 MW.

02 · the daily rhythm

Demand peaks at 6 PM, sleeps at 2 AM.

Averaged across all zones, demand bottoms out at 2 AM (7,665 MW), climbs through the working day, and peaks at 6 PM (10,061 MW) as households come home. The evening peak runs about 31% above the night trough, which is exactly the swing grid operators plan around.

03 · weeks and months

Wednesdays work hardest. January heats most.

Across the week, Wednesday averages the highest load at 9,572 MW and Sunday the lowest at 8,145 MW. Weekends overall run about 12% below weekdays (8,316 vs 9,451 MW), as offices and factories close. Across the six months, January peaks at 10,696 MW on winter heating demand, while May bottoms out at 7,962 MW before air conditioning lifts June back to 8,499 MW.

04 · what the model sees

Nine facts per hour: the clock plus the recent past.

Each hour became a row of 9 features. Six describe position in time: year, month, day, hour, day of week, and a weekend flag. Three carry history: the load one hour ago, 24 hours ago, and 168 hours (one week) ago. Same hour yesterday and same hour last week capture the daily and weekly cycles the charts above reveal.

05 · the models

Random Forest wins by a clear margin.

Four approaches trained on 125,947 hours and faced 31,487 unseen hours. XGBoost reached R² 0.845, while CatBoost and Gradient Boosting landed near 0.76. Random Forest finished first at R² 0.9025 with an average miss of 1,774 MW. It was saved with its metrics and exact feature order for the app, which returned 6,951.78 MW on its sample check.

What the error meansAn average miss of 1,774 MW sounds large until it is set against loads that average 9,125 MW and swing past 10,000 MW at peak. In relative terms the typical miss is under one fifth of demand, which is workable for hour-ahead planning and a clear step up from the 2,921 MW miss of the runner-up.
06 · what to do with it

An hour of warning is worth a lot of power.

An hour-ahead load forecast lets grid planners line up generation before the evening peak instead of reacting to it. Peaker plants, which cost the most to run, can be scheduled for the hours the model flags, like the 6 PM climb, and stood down for the 2 AM trough. The Streamlit app puts the same model behind a simple form: enter a date and hour, get the expected load.

the bottom line

Demand keeps office hours. The model learned the schedule.

European electricity demand peaks at 6 PM, rests at 2 AM, works weekdays, and heats in January. Those rhythms repeat so reliably that recent history plus the clock predicts the next hour well: a Random Forest explains 90.25% of load variance with a 1,774 MW average miss.

Know when demand rises, know by how much, and have the power ready before it does.
