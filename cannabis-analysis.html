Cannabis Gross Sales Prediction

I had four years of daily cannabis sales in front of me and one question I actually cared about: if I know everything sold up to today, can I call tomorrow's number? What followed was the full journey from raw records to a working forecast, with one result I did not see coming.

01 · the data

Four complete years, zero gaps.

The dataset covers 1,461 daily records, January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2024. I counted it out: that is exactly every calendar day across those four years, leap day included. Nothing skipped. Each row holds the date, the day's gross sales, which product category sold, and the day of the week.

And for once, the data needed no rescue work:

The records come from Massachusetts Cannabis Control Commission public sales data, and a duplicate check came back with zero duplicates. Across all 1,461 days, the average day brought in about $201,700, and the middle day sits at almost exactly the same number, so daily sales spread out evenly instead of piling up at one end.

02 · sales over time

Sales more than doubled in four years.

The totals climbed every single year, from about $45.7M in 2021 to $100.5M in 2024. No down years, no drama:

Zoom in and the same story repeats with natural wiggles. January 2021 opened at $3.52M. That December closed at $4.52M. By December 2024, a single month pulled in $8.89M. The weakest month in the entire dataset was February 2021 at just $2.58M. The line points up and to the right, and that slope later became one of my best forecasting clues.

04 · preparing the forecast

Teaching the model to look backward.

One day of sales cannot predict the next day by itself. So I gave every day a memory, built from two kinds of clues: calendar clues (which month, which weekday, weekend or not, start or end of the month) and history clues (what actually sold recently).

The history clues are simple ideas:

  • Yesterday's sales (lag_1): what sold just one day ago
  • Last week's sales (lag_7): the same weekday, one week back
  • Last month's sales (lag_30): the same date, roughly a month back
  • Weekly and monthly averages (rolling means): the average of the last 7 or 30 days, which smooths out one-off spikes

06 · fair testing

Train on the past, test on the future.

Here is where many forecasts quietly cheat: shuffle the days randomly and the model gets to peek at the future while training. The score looks great and means nothing. So I split the data in calendar order instead:

SetDaysPeriod
Training1,144Jan 31, 2021 to Mar 19, 2024
Testing287Mar 20, 2024 to Dec 31, 2024

The first 30 days went to setup duty, since the 30-day history clues need a month of runway before they exist. After that, the model learned from the past and then faced almost ten months of future it had never seen. No peeking.

As one more honesty check, both linear models also went through 5-fold time-series cross-validation on the training data, averaging R² of about 0.70 across folds. Lower than the final test score, which is normal: the early folds train on far less history.

08 · why simple won

The data was smooth. The trees wanted bumps.

Here is my read on what happened. These sales move in smooth, steady relationships. Yesterday looks like today, the averages glide, the trend climbs. Straight-line math follows that shape naturally. Tree models work differently: they chop data into boxes, and boxes are a bad way to extend a steady climb into months you have never seen. So they stumbled.

Ridge and Linear finished in a genuine dead heat, one dollar apart on the average miss. Ridge won on paper ($4,444 against $4,445), but I drew the final forecast charts with Linear Regression. Same picture, far easier to explain to anyone.

09 · what the business learns

Five takeaways for anyone selling.

  • Growth is real: $45.7M a year became $100.5M in four years.
  • Watch yesterday: recent sales carry almost all the forecasting signal.
  • Flower leads but nothing dominates: no single category carries the business.
  • Q4 is always biggest: plan stock and staff around the final quarter.
  • Simple beats complex here: judge models on unseen data, never on reputation.
the bottom line

History predicts. Simplicity wins.

Start to finish, this ran the whole pipeline: raw records, validation, exploration, history-based features, honest chronological testing, a six-way model contest, and a business reading at the end. Headline score: R² 0.900 with an average miss of $4,444 a day.

The number is nice, but the lesson is better. Recent history carried almost all the signal, and the plainest model in the room captured it best. That is a template any business can reuse on its own sales.

The best model is decided by performance on unseen data, not by how complicated the algorithm is.
