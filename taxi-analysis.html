NYC Taxi Fare & Revenue Analysis

How much revenue does a taxi network generate in a month, when is demand highest, and can the total fare of a trip be predicted before it is completed? To answer these questions, I analyzed 3,475,204 NYC taxi trips recorded during January 2025, combining data cleaning, exploratory analysis, feature engineering, and machine learning.

chapter 01

Preparing 3.47 Million Taxi Trips

Working with more than three million records required careful data cleaning before any analysis or modeling. I examined the dataset for missing values, duplicates, invalid values, and inconsistent records.

Five columns each had 540,149 missing values (15.5% of rows): passenger count, rate code, store-and-forward flag, congestion surcharge, and airport fee. Rather than blindly filling them, I examined the structure of the missing records first, then filled passenger count with the median and the remaining columns with their modes or medians as appropriate.

chapter 02

How Much Revenue Did NYC Taxi Trips Generate?

$90,335,708.80Total taxi revenue across 3,475,204 January 2025 trips, at an average fare of $25.99.

This immediately demonstrated the scale of the transportation system in the dataset. But total revenue alone does not tell us when or how that revenue was generated, so I analyzed revenue and trip volume across different time periods.

chapter 03

When Was Taxi Demand Highest?

The clearest pattern appeared when trips were grouped by hour. The highest trip volume occurred around 6 PM with more than 253,000 trips, which fits the evening commute as the working day ends and evening activity begins.

Interestingly, the hour with the highest average fare was different: 5 AM at $25.67 average fare. This demonstrates an important distinction between demand volume and trip value. An hour can generate many trips without producing the highest average fare. For a transportation company, these are two different business questions:

When do we need the most vehicles? And when are individual trips most valuable?

Daily Revenue Patterns

chapter 05

Weekday vs Weekend Behavior

Weekday transportation dominates the network, consistent with a system driven by commuting and regular weekday activity. Weekend trips remain significant and may have different demand characteristics, which matters for vehicle availability, driver scheduling, and demand forecasting.

chapter 06

Does the Day of the Week Affect Fare Value?

The highest average fare occurred on Monday at $21.39. Trip frequency and trip value do not necessarily move together, and understanding that distinction helps transportation companies make better operational decisions.

chapter 07

Building a Fare Prediction Model

After the exploratory analysis, I moved from understanding the data to predicting the total fare of an individual trip. The target variable was total_amount, using trip and time information available about each ride: passenger count, trip distance, rate code, pickup and drop-off locations, payment type, store-and-forward flag, plus engineered time features.

Avoiding leakage. Fare components that directly add up to the total (fare amount, tip, tolls, taxes, surcharges, fees) were removed from the inputs, so the model learns pricing patterns instead of arithmetic. The target was also capped at $300 and trips limited to plausible distance and passenger ranges.

The data was split 80/20 into train and test sets, and categorical time features were one-hot encoded. The objective was simple:

Can we estimate the final taxi fare using information available about the trip?

chapter 09

Why LightGBM Performed Best

Taxi fares contain nonlinear relationships. A $5 increase in trip distance does not produce the same fare increase for every type of trip, and fare behavior shifts with rate code, location, distance, passenger count, time, and payment type. Tree-based boosting algorithms capture these interactions well: LightGBM builds many decision trees sequentially, each new tree correcting errors made by previous trees, learning complex patterns without requiring every interaction to be specified by hand.

Two checks backed up the result. Trip distance alone correlated with the total fare at 0.92, confirming distance as the dominant signal. And the final model scored R² 0.950 on training data versus 0.948 on test data, showing it learned general patterns rather than memorizing trips.

chapter 10

From Prediction to Business Value

The model has applications beyond simply predicting a fare:

Detect unusual fares

If the predicted fare differs significantly from the recorded fare, the trip can be flagged for investigation.

Improve fare estimation

Estimated fares can be provided to passengers before or during a journey.

Support revenue analysis

The model helps identify which combinations of distance, location, time, and rate type contribute most strongly to fare value.

Improve operational planning

Combined with demand analysis, fare predictions help operators understand not only where demand is high, but also where and when trips generate greater revenue.

final findings

Volume and value peak at different times.

The network generated approximately $90.34 million across more than 3.47 million trips. Demand concentrated during the day, with 6 PM recording the highest trip volume, yet the highest average fare occurred around 5 AM. Weekdays dominated volume, while single days such as January 16 generated exceptional revenue.

On modeling, nonlinear tree-based models beat the linear approach clearly, with LightGBM at MAE $2.61, RMSE $4.57, and R² 0.948. The project combined descriptive analytics, predictive analytics, and business intelligence in one pipeline.

A large dataset is not valuable simply because it contains millions of rows. The real value comes from turning those records into information that supports better decisions.
