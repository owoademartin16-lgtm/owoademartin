Fraud Detection & Risk Mitigation in Nigerian Banking Transactions

Nigeria's retail banks processed 5,000,000 transactions worth ₦328.11 billion between January 2023 and December 2024. 40,000 of them were fraudulent, worth ₦2.66 billion. I wanted to know where that loss concentrates, what separates it from legitimate traffic, and what a risk team should do about it.

01 · the data

Five million rows, sixteen columns, one fraud flag.

The dataset is a public release of Nigerian retail banking transactions. It covers the transaction, the customer and account, the device, the merchant and the location. The target is a single boolean, fraud_flag.

The data was clean: no missing values, no duplicate rows. It was heavy, at 1.1 GB in memory. Downcasting the three monetary columns to 32-bit floats and converting the repeated strings to categorical types brought it to 741.5 MB, which made every groupby fast enough to iterate on.

question 01 · when does fraud happen

Fraud runs on the night shift. Daylight is almost clean.

I grouped every transaction by hour of day and calculated the fraud rate inside each hour. The result is the sharpest pattern in the data.

Nine clock hours, 22:00 through 06:59, hold 39,634 of the 40,000 cases. The rate peaks at 02:00 (8.03%), 04:00 (7.99%) and 22:00 (7.76%). The other fifteen hours share 366 cases, at rates from 0.0036% to 0.0132%.

22:00 alone accounts for 12,924 cases, or 32.3% of all fraud, because it is both a high-rate hour and a high-volume one. The overnight window carries 515,882 transactions, 10.3% of volume, and almost every fraud in the file.

question 02 · do day and month matter

Weekdays and months are flat. Only the hour moves.

I tested the other time dimensions to see whether the effect belonged to the clock or to some larger cycle. It belongs to the clock.

Monday 0.807%, Tuesday 0.779%, Wednesday 0.809%, Thursday 0.801%, Friday 0.804%, Saturday 0.793%, Sunday 0.806%. The full spread is 0.03 percentage points.

Monthly rates move between 0.765% (July 2024) and 0.847% (February 2024), 0.08 points around a 0.80% average. Monthly losses follow volume, not risk: ₦87.1M in February 2023, ₦132.9M in September 2024.

Look only at the calendar and fraud looks uniform. Look at the clock and it is not.

question 04 · which states carry the risk

Bayelsa has the highest rate. Kaduna loses the most money.

Bayelsa records 1.134%, about 1.4 times the national average, on 6,347 transactions and 72 cases. Gombe (0.987%) and Nasarawa (0.867%) follow, also on small bases. Eight of the ten states sit under 50,000 transactions, where a handful of cases moves the rate.

Amount, type and merchant category separate nothing.

question 06 · recommendations

Five controls drawn straight from the numbers.

  1. Guard the night window. 22:00 to 06:59, all channels: biometric or OTP plus a device-binding check. Nine hours, 10.3% of volume, 99.1% of fraud.
  2. Delay high-value mobile and POS transfers. Above ₦50,000: a five-minute hold and a callback. The two channels carry 65.1% of losses.
  3. Watch Kaduna and Edo for money, Bayelsa and Gombe for rate. Pool small states into regional rules so 72 cases do not set policy.
  4. Put agent, branch and USSD on an exception report. They run above 0.82% on low volume and carry little telemetry.
  5. Review the night window every quarter. If transaction mix or monitoring coverage shifts, narrow or widen it on evidence rather than leaving it fixed.

Do not build rules on amount, transaction type or merchant category. They are flat in this data and will add friction without detection.

the bottom line

Fraud is 0.80% of transactions and 99.1% of the problem.

Across five million transactions and ₦328.11 billion in value, fraud holds at 0.80% in every month, every weekday, every channel, every amount band and almost every merchant category. Split the same data by hour and the rate is 7.75% overnight against 0.01% in daylight.

Channel and geography decide how much is at risk. Mobile and POS carry 65.1% of losses; Kaduna and Edo hold 5.75%. The hour decides when the risk is realised, and nothing else in the dataset comes close.

The useful negative result is that the usual suspects do not work. Amount, transaction type and merchant category should not carry a rule on their own.

Fraud is not a large-transaction problem. It is an after-hours problem.
