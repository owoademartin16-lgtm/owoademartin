London Fire Brigade Incident Analysis

When does London call the fire brigade, and for what? I worked with 358,338 incident records from January 2024 onward in PostgreSQL to map demand by category, borough, hour, weekday, and year. The headline finding: only about 1 in 7 calls is a fire.

01 · the data

One download, two tables, 39 columns.

The records come from the London Datastore release covering 2024 onward: incident dates and times, categories, property details, boroughs, coordinates, attendance times, stations, pump counts, and estimated costs. I loaded the source into PostgreSQL and kept two tables: lfb_incidents_raw preserves the original import untouched, while lfb_incidents_clean carries the prepared analysis data. Nothing was overwritten.

Validation came first. All 358,338 rows carry an incident number with zero duplicate rows. Missing incident groups (150) became Unknown. Latitude and longitude are missing in 221,313 rows (61.8%), which limits map-based analysis. First-pump attendance time is missing in 18,654 rows, and those were kept as missing rather than filled with zero, since no measurement is not the same as an instant response. Checked numeric fields held no negative values.

One scope note for every chart below. The data runs from January 2024 to July 2026, about two and a half years. August to December figures therefore rest on two years while January to July rest on nearly three, so cross-month totals are not directly comparable.

02 · what london calls about

Most calls are not fires.

False alarms lead at 164,095 incidents (45.79%), with special services next at 145,693 (40.66%). Together that is 86.45% of all records. Actual fires total 48,400, or 13.51%. Judging brigade workload by fires alone misses six sevenths of the work.

03 · the daily rhythm

Demand bottoms at 5 AM and peaks at 6 PM.

Calls fall overnight to 6,244 at 05:00, climb all day, and top out at 21,341 at 18:00 before easing into the night. Each category follows the same curve with its own peak: false alarms hit 9,327 at 18:00, fires 3,530 at 18:00, and special services 8,891 at 17:00. Staffing that ignores this curve will be thin exactly when London calls most.

04 · days and boroughs

Saturday leads by total. Per day, weekends edge it.

Saturday recorded the most incidents at 52,419 and Sunday the fewest at 49,845, with weekdays spread between 50,546 and 52,119. Adjusted for the 675 weekdays and 268 weekend days observed, weekends average 381.58 incidents per day against 379.37 on weekdays, a gap of 0.58%. The difference is real but small, so it should not drive staffing on its own without further testing.

Westminster counts most, with a warning attached

Westminster recorded 23,332 incidents (6.51%), far ahead of Lambeth at 16,039 and Southwark at 15,848. But these are raw counts. Population, daytime visitors, building density, and land use all differ by borough, and none are adjusted for here. Westminster is the right place to start investigating, not a verdict on risk.

05 · months across years

2026 runs hot in every month so far.

January through July 2026 each beat the same month in 2025: January +7.20%, February +7.36%, March +4.30%, April +6.43%, May +14.38%, June +7.62%, July +12.66%. May and July stand out. This pattern deserves monitoring, but it is not proof of a long-term rise: reporting coverage for each month must be confirmed first, and August to December 2026 are not in the data yet.

06 · response and gaps

Fires get the fastest first pump, barely.

First-pump attendance averages 341 seconds for fires, 338 for special services, and 317 for false alarms, with medians of 318, 321, and 298 seconds. The gaps between categories are small, around 20 seconds. Any response-time comparison must report valid observation counts: 162,748 false alarms, 129,059 special services, and 47,858 fires carried a measured time.

Two data gaps limit what can be claimed. Coordinates are missing in 221,313 rows (61.8%), so precise mapping is off the table and borough analysis carries the geography instead. Attendance times are missing in 18,654 rows. Both gaps should be tracked by incident group and date to check whether they cluster anywhere.

07 · recommendations

Five next steps, in priority order.

  1. Staff the 17:00 to 19:00 window first. Demand looks highest between 5 PM and 7 PM, so check whether staffing and vehicles cover those hours. Do not judge by raw totals alone. Look at average demand on a typical day, how long crews take to arrive, and how many appliances are free. One hundred calls spread over a month is a different problem from one hundred calls in a single evening.
  2. Take false alarms apart. They are the largest category, so break them down by property type, location, and time of day to see which ones could be prevented. A shop or office block that triggers repeated false alarms may point to faulty detectors or poor maintenance, but the data must carry those details before anyone names a cause.
  3. Adjust borough counts for exposure. A borough with the most incidents is not automatically the riskiest. More residents, buildings, businesses, and daytime visitors will naturally produce more calls. Compare counts against population, building density, and daytime footfall, for example incidents per 10,000 residents, before ranking boroughs or directing resources.
  4. Keep watching 2026 month by month. Track each month against the same month last year, but first confirm the data is complete. A partial year cannot be fairly compared with a full one, and late or missing records can fake a trend. Only call it growth when coverage checks out.
  5. Monitor the missing data. Check whether gaps cluster anywhere: coordinates missing more in some boroughs, attendance times missing more in busy periods or certain months. Gaps like these are rarely random, and dropping those rows quietly can bias everything built on top of them.
the bottom line

Most calls are not fires. Evenings are busiest. 2026 bears watching.

False alarms and special services make up 86.45% of records. Demand peaks at 18:00 and barely moves between weekdays and weekends. Westminster leads on raw counts with the usual caveats. January through July 2026 each beat 2025, led by May at +14.38%. Missing coordinates and attendance times set the limits on mapping and response analysis.

Count everything, adjust for exposure, and fix the data gaps that block the next question.
